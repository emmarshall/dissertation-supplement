Deviations

For each departure, I recorded when I chose it, whether I had seen the data, and the analytic discretion it allowed. Following Lakens (2024), I assessed test severity separately from the validity of the inference. Severity concerns how likely a test is to support a true hypothesis and to withhold support from a false one. A departure can leave severity unchanged, reduce it, or increase it; improving validity does not necessarily make a test more severe.

The five overlapping categories are unforeseen events, mistakes in the preregistration or study, missing information, violations of untested assumptions, and falsified auxiliary hypotheses. Each entry assesses severity, and entries involving a mistake, violated assumption, or falsified auxiliary hypothesis also assess validity. I retained the decision rules in the preregistration and its supplement.

1. E4a Sensitivity Analysis

Registered on 2026-09-21. Added on 2026-09-25, while Study 1 data collection was under way and before any relevance rating had been analyzed.

What was registered. E4a concerns whether self-managed abortion (SMA) evidence increases the relevance of outcome-consistent items more than inconsistent items. Its parameter is the SMA × Consistency interaction in relevance ~ outcome × consistency + legal context + attitudes + (1 + consistency | participant) + (1 | item), with items 1, 6, and 7 coded consistent and the other seven coded inconsistent (analysis supplement, §E4a).

What I added. I reran E4a without the employment item (3) and the age and household item (10), using the same model and decision rule on the remaining eight items. The ten-item analysis remains the registered result; I report the sensitivity analysis beside it.

Why. In Pilot 2, items 3 and 10 received the lowest relevance ratings, changed little with the search outcome, and formed a separate background factor in every vignette. Study 1 largely retained the wording and consistency coding from the non-criminal miscarriage vignette, where both items were coded inconsistent. The E4a contrast could therefore partly compare information about the loss with background details. In that Pilot 2 vignette, the raw difference-in-differences was +0.87 with the background items and +0.50 without them (Pilot 2 deviations record, §9.2). I used the eight-item version to check E4a’s dependence on those items.

Category. Not a deviation. I added a sensitivity analysis based only on Pilot 2, before Study 1 data collection ended and before I saw any Study 1 relevance rating. The registered analysis and its severity are unchanged.

2. Substitute Mediator (H3c–H3e)

Registered on 2026-09-21. Recorded on 2026-09-26, after Study 1 data collection ended and before any Study 1 outcome had been analyzed.

What was registered. H3c–H3e test whether outcome information relates to blame of the suspect indirectly, “through how likely participants think it is that the suspect committed the offense.” The indirect effect was predicted to be positive after SMA evidence (H3c) and negative after financial-crime evidence (H3d), each compared with no outcome information. H3e predicts a more positive indirect effect after SMA than financial-crime evidence. I specified separate groups for the two legal contexts, adjusting each equation for the four attitudes. Percentile 95% bootstrap intervals (10,000 samples) evaluate the indirect effects and their difference; robust Wald intervals describe the individual paths. The model is estimated again without the attitudes, and support is read on the indirect effects alone (analysis supplement, “Probability and blame (H3c–H3e)” and Figure 4). The registered mediator is a single item rated from 0 to 100: “Based on the evidence available at the time the warrant was sought, what is the probability that the suspect committed the crime the detective suspects her of committing?”

What happened. A technical glitch prevented the survey from displaying the probability-of-guilt question, leaving no responses to the registered mediator.

What the analysis does instead. I retained the H3c–H3e model, intervals, and decision rules but supplied pregnancy-evidence likelihood as the mediator. This registered 0–100 composite combines the likelihood ratings for abortion and disposal evidence and is also the mediator in E9a–E9b. S7 reports the analysis as a substitute. Chapter 5 reports H3a–H3b’s registered blame comparisons and explains in a footnote why H3c–H3e could not be tested as registered.

Why this measure. I chose pregnancy-evidence likelihood because a judgment that the search would uncover evidence of an offense bears on whether the suspect committed it, the relationship posited in H3c–H3e. Foreseeability measures predictability from the detective’s perspective; E9 specifies no directional prediction through it. Pregnancy-evidence likelihood addresses the related likelihood question, but it does not directly measure the probability of guilt.

Category. A mistake in the study: the survey omitted a registered item. I chose the existing pregnancy-evidence measure after data collection ended but before analyzing any Study 1 outcome or examining a likelihood or blame value. I considered no other candidate. With the registered mediator unavailable, I cannot assess the severity of that test. I report the substitute’s verdicts only for the related question it answers.

3. Evidence-Relevance Structure

Registered on 2026-09-21. Recorded on 2026-10-01, after the Study 1 data had been analyzed.

What was registered. The measurement procedure chooses a factor structure for the ten relevance ratings with parallel analysis, a screen that requires at least three items per factor, and registered fit criteria. If no structure qualifies, the analyses use the ten-item mean. The procedure makes no provision for removing items. With more than one factor, each factor is a separate mediator, and the decision reads the sum across factors (analysis supplement, measurement and H1 sections).

What happened. No structure qualified, so every registered analysis uses the ten-item mean, and those results stand. The three-factor pattern failed the screen because its third factor had only two items. Both items described the suspect’s background. Item 3 said that she had held a stable job for several years, and item 10 said that she was 32 years old and lived with her husband.

What I added. Without items 3 and 10, the other eight items formed two factors. Items 1, 6, and 7 formed one factor, and items 2, 4, 5, 8, and 9 formed the other. This is the item-consistency key of E4a. I adopted this structure because of the content of the items, not by the registered rule, and it fails the registered fit criteria. I refit the six structural models that use the relevance mediator with this structure (H1a–H1d and E1a–E1b; H2a–H2b; E6a–E6f; E12a–E12d; H4b–H4d). These analyses applied the registered decision rules to the sum across factors, with 10,000 bootstrap attempts. I made no decision for E12a–E12c because fewer attempts were usable than the registration requires. These supplementary results accompany the registered results without replacing them. All Study 1 analyses used 10,000 bootstrap attempts, including this analysis.

Category. A violation of an untested assumption (i.e., that each indicated factor would have at least three qualifying items). I decided on this analysis after the data had been analyzed, so it tests the hypotheses less severely than the registered analysis.

4. Relative Relevance (H1; H4b–H4d)

Registered on 2026-09-21. Decided on 2026-10-01, after the Study 1 data had been analyzed.

What was registered. H1 and H4b–H4d predict that outcome information changes the relevance of fitting evidence relative to non-fitting evidence. The structural models use evidence relevance as the mediator, which the registered measurement procedure made the ten-item mean (see item 3). Only E4a tests the relative item-level change directly, and that model has no likelihood outcome.

What the analysis does instead. In the Chapter 5 H1 and H4b–H4d analyses, I supplied relative relevance as the observed mediator: the mean for items consistent with self-managed abortion (i.e., items 1, 6, and 7, as coded for E4a) minus the mean for the remaining items. I used the ten-item key for the primary analysis and omitted background items 3 and 10 for the sensitivity analysis (see item 1). An added step supplied the mediator to the unchanged registered code. Models, samples, grouping and constraints, covariates, missing-data handling, the 10,000-attempt participant bootstrap, and decision rules remained as registered. Because the key codes fit to the self-managed abortion outcome only, relative relevance does not test sense-making after financial-crime evidence (H1b and H4c). The registered results based on the ten-item mean for all of these hypotheses, and the two-factor analysis from item 3, are reported in this supplement.

Why. The consistent-minus-inconsistent mean measures the relative change even when the mean across all ten items stays constant or falls.

Category. A mistake in the preregistration. I could have recognized the mismatch between the mean and the predicted quantity before collecting data, but chose the replacement only after seeing the registered results. This choice reduced the severity of the relative-relevance tests, although it improved construct validity. Their H1a–H1d and H4b–H4d decisions may differ from the registered decisions.

5. Exploratory Suspect Judgments

Registered on 2026-09-21. Tests added on 2026-10-01, after I had seen the Study 1 results.

What was registered. H3a–H3b compare suspect blame across outcome conditions using an Outcome × Legal Context analysis of covariance; E7a–E7g use that model for punishment, recklessness, and negligence. E8b jointly models responsibility and the wrongness of the suspect’s conduct with blame and punishment, while the sample descriptions report that wrongness rating by condition (analysis supplement, “Sample descriptions and evidence measurement”). The registration provides no test of causality, preventability, favorable impression, justified reasons, wrongness apart from the law, wrongness given the charge, or counterfactual availability (study materials, sections H and I). Earlier E6a–E6c plans covered causality, preventability, and favorable impression but were withdrawn before registration.

What I added. After seeing the Study 1 results, I added tests on 2026-10-01 for nine judgments: causality, preventability, favorable impression, justified reasons, wrongness apart from the law, wrongness given the charge, counterfactual availability, the wrongness of the suspect’s conduct, and responsibility. I used the H3a–H3b blame model, substituting each judgment as the outcome. For each, I compared expected evidence with no information, unexpected evidence with no information, and expected with unexpected evidence, weighting the two legal contexts equally. The comparisons use Student t 95% intervals from the conventional covariance, with and without the four attitudes, in the analysis sample (N = 398) and registered sensitivity sample (N = 341). Although E8b includes responsibility and wrongness, its joint model gives normal Wald intervals; these additional tests give the blame model’s t intervals. The model with the registered covariates and sample adds 27 tests (i.e., nine judgments with three comparisons each), and the three companion versions add 81. I applied no multiplicity correction, consistent with the registered single-judgment comparisons.

Why. I added the pairwise comparisons to report judgments of the women consistently across studies.

Category. Not a deviation. These exploratory additions carry no letter or decision and do not alter any registered model, rule, test, or verdict. I chose them with the results in view, so their severity is low; the severity of the registered tests is unchanged.
