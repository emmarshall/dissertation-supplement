Analytic Framework and Hypotheses

November 18, 2026
Emma Marshall, J.D., M.A.
University of Nebraska-Lincoln

Analytic Framework.

I conducted the analyses in R (R Core Team, 2026), using Bayesian multilevel models in brms (Bürkner, 2017, 2018) to account for the repeated ratings within participants. Non-criminal miscarriage was the reference scenario. I computed planned contrasts with marginaleffects (Arel-Bundock et al., 2024) and summarized indirect effects from the posterior draws (Falk et al., 2023; Makowski et al., 2019; Miočević et al., 2018; Vuorre & Bolger, 2018). I assessed the effects using their 95% credible intervals (Kruschke & Liddell, 2018).

The preregistration allowed frequentist mixed factorial ANOVAs of the continuous outcomes. The analysis plan also included a GEE check of search-target endorsement. I evaluated the registered hypotheses with the Bayesian decision rules. This supplement reports the Bayesian results.

The multilevel models take the following form. \(Y\) is a continuous outcome measured on occasion \(t\) (i.e., before or after the outcome, or the vignette) within participant \(j\). The kind of incriminating evidence found, \(\text{Outcome}_j\), varies between participants:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Level 1:} \quad & Y_{tj} = \pi_{0j} + \pi_{1j}\,W_{tj} + e_{tj}, \quad e_{tj} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\ \sigma^{2}) \\ \text{Level 2:} \quad & \pi_{0j} = \gamma_{00} + \gamma_{01}\,\text{Outcome}_{j} + u_{0j} \\ & \pi_{1j} = \gamma_{10} + \gamma_{11}\,\text{Outcome}_{j} + u_{1j} \\ & (u_{0j},\ u_{1j})^{\top} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\ \mathbf{T}). \end{aligned} \]

Here \(W_{tj}\) is the within-participant factor (i.e., before or after the outcome, or the vignette). The within-participant change is represented by \(\gamma_{10}\), with \(\gamma_{11}\) representing how that change depends on the kind of evidence found. Participant intercepts and slopes have covariance matrix \(\mathbf{T}\). For binary search-target endorsement, I retained the Level-2 structure and modeled \(\mathrm{logit}\big(\mathrm{Pr}(Y_{tj} = 1)\big)\) in place of \(Y_{tj}\), without a Level-1 residual term.

I used mean-zero Normal priors for the regression coefficients, with widths matched to the outcome scales to provide weak regularization. The log-odds coefficients of the binary-choice models had a similarly weak prior (Bürkner, 2018; Gelman et al., 2008; Gelman et al., 2014; Hox et al., 2018; Sorensen et al., 2016). I used an LKJ(2) prior for correlations among participant random effects and retained the scale-aware Student-t defaults in brms for intercepts, random-effect standard deviations, and residual standard deviations (Bürkner, 2017, 2018). For the H5a sensitivity check, I refit the model with half and twice the coefficient-prior widths (Depaoli et al., 2020; Hox et al., 2018). Table P2.4 gives the prior for each parameter class.

For H5a, E5.1, E5.2 and E6.4, the first-stage mediator equations used one participant mean per person; the later equations retained the scenario ratings.

I fit the brms models with four chains per data set (Bürkner, 2017, 2018). The default chain length was 4,000 iterations, including 1,000 warmup, with longer chains when needed for convergence. The primary H5a and matched-item models used five completed data sets and 32,000 iterations per chain, including 8,000 warmup. I required R̂ < 1.01, bulk and tail effective sample sizes > 400, and no divergent transitions (Vehtari et al., 2021).
