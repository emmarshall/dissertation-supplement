Verdicts, Mental State and Legal Accuracy

November 18, 2026
Emma Marshall, J.D., M.A.
University of Nebraska-Lincoln

This chapter reports the analyses of the narratives by verdict condition (RQ2, H2a, H2b, and H2c) and three exploratory analyses. These are the accuracy of participants’ legal judgments, the three-way interaction of abortion attitudes, state law, and judgment type, and the comparison of the narratives with television plotlines and documented cases.

Narrative Features by Verdict Condition (RQ2)

A series of descriptive chi-square tests compared the frequency of each feature across the four verdict conditions, with the Benjamini–Hochberg correction applied across the testable features (RQ2; see Table P1.16). After correction, the four verdict conditions differed on 12 narrative features. These included legal terminology, \(\chi^2(3, N = 311) = 28.98\), BH-adjusted p < .001. For causal structure, the association with verdict condition was Cramér’s V = .29.

Coded Factors by Verdict Condition (H2a and H2b)

H2a predicted that narratives written toward a wrongful verdict would contain more coded mitigating and aggravating factors than narratives written toward a rightful verdict. There was no significant difference in total coded factors per narrative across the four verdict conditions, F(3, 76) = 0.88, MSe = 0.33, p = .456, \(\eta^2\) = .034. Contrary to H2a, narratives in the wrongful conditions (M = 1.00, SD = 0.56) did not contain significantly more coded factors than narratives in the rightful conditions (M = 0.80, SD = 0.57; see Figure P1.3).

H2b predicted that the wrongful–rightful difference would be larger for mitigating than for aggravating factors. There was no significant interaction between verdict condition and factor type, F(3, 76) = 0.59, MSe = 0.13, p = .622, \(\eta_p^2\) = .023. Contrary to H2b, the wrongful–rightful difference was not larger for mitigating factors. However, there was a significant main effect of factor type: narratives contained more mitigating factors (M = 0.50, SD = 0.37) than aggravating factors (M = 0.39, SD = 0.39; F(1, 76) = 4.54, MSe = 0.13, p = .036, \(\eta_p^2\) = .056).

Mental State by Actual Culpability (H2c)

H2c rests on the coded mental-state category, so I report it as exploratory. H2c predicted that narratives in the culpable conditions would attribute purposeful or knowing mental states more often than narratives in the non-culpable conditions. The relationship between actual culpability and coded mental state was not significant, \(\chi^2\)(1, N = 279) = 0.32, p = .572. Contrary to H2c, coded mental state did not differ by actual culpability. However, the mental-state code did not meet the preregistered reliability target (ICC = .67, registered \(\kappa\) = .43, target = .80; see Table P1.5), leaving this result inconclusive. The code was not reliable enough to tell whether participants matched the mental state to the verdict they wrote toward or to the facts of the case.

Legal Accuracy of the Crime Judgments

State-law coding. For the legal-accuracy sub-analysis, participants’ descriptive judgments (“Is this a crime where you live?”) were compared with the legal status of each scenario in their state of residence as of May 2026. Participant responses were matched based on accuracy (yes/no). However, given that the specifics of state laws regarding abortion remain in flux and convoluted, I did not interpret a mismatch as true ignorance of the underlying state-specific status. For example, many state abortion bans do not apply to pregnant people as a matter of black letter law, but they still can be, and have been, used to criminalize abortion (Weigel et al., 2020), so whether self-managed abortion is a crime in a given state is not a clear binary. The coding rules, sources, and confidence flags used in the preregistered sensitivity analysis appear in State-Law Coding for the Legal-Accuracy Sub-Analysis and Table P1.17.

State-Law Coding for the Legal-Accuracy Sub-Analysis

The legal-accuracy sub-analysis compared each participant’s answer to the descriptive question (“Is this a crime where you live?”) with the current legal status of each of the 20 scenarios in the participant’s reported state. To do this I drew on several existing sources. I used the Center for Reproductive Rights’ “After Roe Fell” map as of May 2026 (Center for Reproductive Rights, 2026), Pregnancy Justice’s After Pregnancy Loss report (Pregnancy Justice, 2026), the NCSL’s fetal-homicide compilation (National Conference of State Legislatures, 2018), and the appellate case law in Aspinwall (2023), Bach & Wasilczuk (2025), and Darlington et al. (2021).

Note. Crime in lists the jurisdictions where I coded the scenario as a crime (1); I coded it 0 elsewhere. High, medium, and low confidence describe how firmly the statutes and case law supported the coding.

For scenarios involving inchoate conduct, I assigned low confidence because the legal status was unclear. The miscarriage-management scenarios and home disposal after a miscarriage are not defined as crimes in their own right. I therefore coded them under the general abuse-of-corpse or concealment statutes (Pregnancy Justice, 2026; Rinkunas, 2026). I assigned medium confidence to the substance-use scenario on the basis of appellate decisions in Alabama, South Carolina, and Oklahoma (Aspinwall, 2023). Most cases with known pregnancy outcomes in the documented sample involved live births, however, limiting their relevance to the fetal-death scenario (Bach & Wasilczuk, 2025). Confidence was also medium for the accident scenario and low for the omission-of-care scenarios. Most fetal-homicide statutes exempt the pregnant woman (National Conference of State Legislatures, 2018; Unborn Victims of Violence Act of 2004, 2004), and no statute makes delaying or failing to report care a crime on its own (Pregnancy Justice, 2026).

I report the legal-accuracy analysis as exploratory. Compared with the state-law coding, accuracy differed significantly from chance on 19 of the 20 scenarios. Accuracy on the 13 state-invariant scenarios ranged from .70 to 1.00 (M = .96) and was lower on average on the six abortion scenarios with between-state variance (.64 to .82, M = .76). The per-scenario accuracy tallies appear in Table P1.15.

Abortion Attitudes, State Law, and Judgment Type

I report this analysis as exploratory. When the state-law coding was added to the mixed-effects model of card-sort crime judgments, the three-way Attitudes × State Law × Judgment Type interaction was significant (OR = 0.28, 95% CI [0.13, 0.63], z = −3.09, p = .002; see Table P1.13). The pilot was not powered for the three-way interaction, so I report that estimate descriptively.

Comparisons With Television and Documented Cases

Table P1.18 compares the methods specified in the narratives with the procedures visually depicted in U.S. television plotlines (Herold & Sisson, 2019). The automated Section J code found no references to actual cases in the 311 narratives.

I used Pregnancy Justice (2023); the registered television source provided no comparable prosecution figures. For chemical endangerment, I used the criminal child neglect, abuse, or endangerment category (.84 of cases with known charges). I used .06 for fetal homicide and .01 for self-managed abortion. For improper disposal, I added two Pregnancy Justice categories together: tampering with remains or abuse of a corpse (.02) and failure to report a birth or death (.01), for .03. A case could involve charges in more than one category, so the prosecution categories could overlap.

The design assigned about .25 of the prompts to each crime. Relative to the prosecution figures, this gave more weight to fetal homicide, self-managed abortion, and improper disposal, and less to chemical endangerment. The narrative shares therefore reflect that allocation; they do not estimate how common participants thought each crime was.
